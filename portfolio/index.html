What I do

Three ways I'm useful

If your problem doesn't fit neatly in a box, say it in plain words and I'll tell you honestly whether I'm the right person.

01

A website that pulls its weight

Not a brochure nobody reads. A site that loads fast, reads properly on a phone, and that you can edit yourself without calling me every time a price changes. Custom-built, or on WordPress or Shopify if that's what your team already knows.

For businesses whose site is older than their ambitions.

02

The internal tool nobody wants to build

Dashboards, admin panels, back-offices — the software your team lives inside all day. It's rarely glamorous, and it's almost always the thing that saves the most money.

For teams whose real database is a spreadsheet.

03

Automation of the work you repeat

That task somebody does every week, by hand, that has never once been interesting. Data pulled from one place and pushed into another, documents generated, files sorted, reports built. I find it, then I end it.

For anyone who just recognised their own Tuesday.

How it goes

No surprises, in four steps

  1. 01

    We talk for thirty minutes

    You show me the problem. I tell you whether it's worth building, what it would take, and where it would break. Free — and if the numbers don't work, I'll say so instead of selling you something.

  2. 02

    A written scope, a fixed price

    What gets built, what it won't do, what I need from you, and the date it lands. No meter running quietly in the background.

  3. 03

    You see it working early

    A real piece of it, on your real data, before I go deep. That's when changes are still cheap — so that's when I want your opinion.

  4. 04

    You get the keys

    Documented, handed over, walked through with your team, with a support window after launch. It's yours. Nobody should be held hostage by their own software.

About

I came to code the long way round

I studied economics and management, then spent two years running a national platform for a telecom operator — the deadlines, the partners, the phone call at nine on a Friday when something is on fire. That's where I learned the part a lot of developers skip: what a thing is for, and who gets blamed when it doesn't work.

Then I stopped coordinating and started building. Python first, then the web around it. Now I do both — and the project-manager reflex never left. I'll ask what you're actually trying to achieve before I ask what you want me to build.

I work remotely, in English, French or Arabic, and I answer emails like a person.
